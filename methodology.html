How zerostat.ai curates.

A transparent method for tracking AI tools a non-developer might actually use. We publish the scoring dimensions, the data sources, and the 'last checked' date on every entry. We don't aggregate listicles, accept vendor placement, or ship fixed weighted scores dressed as math.

v2 2026-09-25 Rewritten for the brief launch. Previous version described the YouTube-channel ranking rubric; that documentation no longer reflects what this site is.

The short version:

We track AI tools a non-developer small business owner might actually use, across two lanes (general small business, at home). Each tool gets a one-line pitch, an honest take, and five 1–10 scores across capability, cost, reliability, ecosystem, and momentum. There is no weighted composite. Scores are read individually, never multiplied. The honest take is the actual judgment; the scores are for sorting.

Every entry has a last_checked date and at least one source URL. Every claim a vendor could dispute is sourced. Pricing data is checked monthly; entries stale more than 90 days are flagged and re-verified.

The five dimensions

Each entry in the brief carries five scores on a 1–10 scale. They are not combined into a single number. They exist so you can sort the table by what matters to you, and so we can be held to a discrete claim for each axis. If you disagree with a score, argue a specific axis, not vibes.

Capability
Does the tool do the job at a level a non-developer needs? Not benchmarks in isolation — what it actually produces for the kind of user who'd buy it. Output quality, accuracy, depth, format flexibility.
1–10
Cost
Price relative to peers in the same category, including tier structure, hidden costs (usage, seats, add-ons), and the realistic monthly bill for a small business owner. Lower score = more expensive or more confusing pricing.
1–10
Reliability
Uptime, consistency, support quality, hallucination rate where measurable, prompt-to-prompt variance. How often does it break, and how recoverable is it when it does?
1–10
Ecosystem
Integrations, API availability, MCP support, documentation quality, community size. Can you actually plug this into the rest of your stack, or is it a walled garden?
1–10
Momentum
Is the vendor shipping, growing, or drifting? Release cadence, public signal (funding, customer wins, layoffs), category leadership. Lower score = static or declining. Higher score = leader in a fast-moving category.
1–10

Why no weighted composite

An earlier version of this site published fixed weights (30/25/20/15/10) and produced a single composite score. The argument was that weighted math gives the impression of objectivity. The honest version: it doesn't. The weights are opinions dressed as numbers, and any reader who disagrees with a ranking can — and did — argue the coefficients instead of the actual call.

The brief uses 1–10 scores per dimension with no pretense of mathematical precision. The your_take field on each entry is the actual editorial judgment: what we think, in plain language, with reasoning. Scores are for sort and filter. The take is the verdict.

If you want a composite: read the take. If you want to compare: sort by the dimension that matters to you.

Per-lane framing

The brief has two lanes at launch. They use the same five dimensions but emphasize different things.

Small business lane (smb-general)

  • Capability: does it do the job a non-developer actually needs it to do? Customer comms, marketing copy, scheduling, design, video — judged against the real workflow, not benchmark suites.
  • Cost: small business budget pressure is real. A $200/mo Pro tier scores lower here than in a developer context.
  • Ecosystem: integrations to common SMB stacks (Google Workspace, Shopify, QuickBooks, Zapier) matter more than raw API quality.

At-home lane (home-ai)

  • Capability: hardware capability (does the device do what it says), not just software capability. A $99 doorbell with broken person detection scores low even if the spec sheet is good.
  • Cost: total cost of ownership across device + subscription + replacement. Subscription-required devices score lower than one-time-purchase equivalents at similar capability.
  • Reliability: smart home devices that drop off the network are unusable. Higher bar than for software-only tools.
  • Ecosystem: works with the smart home setup the user already has (Alexa, Google Home, HomeKit, SmartThings) is the differentiator.

Where the data comes from

Every claim in the brief traces to one of:

  1. Vendor docs and pricing pages — primary, preferred. The product page, the changelog, the pricing page, the docs.
  2. Independent benchmarks and reviews — when a category has real evaluation work (SWE-bench for coding models, video quality papers, etc.).
  3. Hands-on use — for tools the editor has actually run, not just read about.
  4. Reputable press and analysis — for context, never as the sole source of a numeric claim.

What we don't use:

  • Vendor marketing copy as a source of fact.
  • Aggregator listicles ("top 10 AI tools!") as a source.
  • Single-sourced rumors. If a number is from one place and we can't corroborate, we mark it unconfirmed or skip it.
  • Anything we can't link to in the entry's sources array.

What we publish, and what we hold back

We publish: the rubric, what each axis means, the entry's one-line pitch, the take, the five scores, pricing data, the last_checked date, source URLs.

We hold back: nothing material at present. The dataset is public; the reasoning is in the takes.

What we don't accept: sponsored placements, vendor edits, paid reviews. If a vendor wants to correct a claim, the correction lives in the take or the sources — not in exchange for payment. The brief is independent; we want it to stay credible.

How updates work

The brief runs on three cron jobs: hourly (integrity check + draft review), daily at 09:00 (new-tool discovery), and monthly on the 1st. On the 1st of each month:

  1. The cron fetches every active tool's primary URL and pricing page.
  2. It diffs against the last known state. If the page changed, the tool is flagged for review.
  3. Drafts land in data/drafts/. They are reviewed, not auto-published, until the editor marks the dataset trusted.
  4. Entries with last_checked older than 90 days are re-verified.
  5. A summary of what changed goes to the editor's Telegram (private).

This means the dataset is updated roughly monthly. If you spot an error between updates, the GitHub repo has an issue tracker.

Definitions

Entry
One row in the brief. A single tool with a stable tool_id, a one-line pitch, a take, five scores, pricing data, sources, and a last_checked date.
Lane
The category the entry lives in. Two lanes at launch: smb-general and home-ai.
Take
The your_take field. Two to three sentences in plain language with the editor's honest view. The actual editorial judgment, not a summary of the scores.
Last checked
The date the cron last verified the entry's data. Visible on every card. Older than 90 days triggers a re-verification.
Source
A URL in the entry's sources array. Every numeric or factual claim has at least one source. Sources are not endorsements.
